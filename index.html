The way to the reconcile capability level of AI vs. GDP impact is that the diffusion of AI will take much longer than people think. And it will also show up in ways that are hard to measure in GDP immediately. You could bring the world's greatest superintelligence to many workflows, and still be bound by the laws of corporate physics: getting data prepared and put into a pipeline, process reengineering and change management, aligning on how the new workflow should function, and so on. Even after you solve all that, you're still bound by the speed of the real world: waiting for a customer to respond to a proposal, getting a permit for a project, a drug discovery pipeline taking years to eventually reach the consumer, and so on. Not to mention lots of positive daily AI use-cases are entirely net neutral to GDP, at least in the near term. AI diffusion is going to be the theme of the next decade. The upside is that there's a tremendous amount of opportunity in building the bridges between superintelligence and real-world workflows.

this is a very good and well designed report however, two impt assumptions in it that significantly change the analysis once you start to play with it: 1. the report assumes that jobs can be decomposed into tasks 2. the report models human tasks created by automation always as a fraction of automated tasks. in other words, the underlying assumptions is automation always reduces human labor (or at best leaves it the same) taken in reverse order: does automation always reduce human work? in my experience, the answer is no. automation often creates multiples more work for humans. i have a full argument here (https://t.co/Lb7SUCe2pO) for why this is structurally true. but i think any honest analysis of the economic impacts of AI has to take work creation seriously. can jobs be decomposed into tasks? yes, that is a useful lens. but lists of tasks is a way of looking at jobs—one that makes the question of how much of a job is automatable actually tractable. therefore, it's useful. but this is not the full picture, it's just jobs as exposed to our method of questioning. a good example: under this model, where do the new tasks come from? are they just from bosses that add a list to the task list for workers to complete? where do those tasks come from? unless you can model that well you are missing a key term in the analysis (see the first point above.) another way of looking at this, borrowing from wittgenstein and heidegger, is that a job is a particular way of seeing and caring about the world. an editor notices things a nurse doesn't, and vice versa. what you care about shapes what you notice, what seems worth doing, and what counts as doing it well. you can make some of that explicit as a task list. but as your tools and circumstances change, the same cares can generate entirely new tasks. that process is part of the job. the above is completely missing from this analysis (understandably because it's, by definition, very hard to model!) but if you take it seriously, it shoudl substantially change your perspective automation and jobs

这是一份非常好、设计精良的报告。 不过，其中有两个重要假设，一旦你开始细究，就会显著改变整个分析： 1. 报告假设「工作」可以被拆解为「任务」。 2. 报告把「自动化所创造的人类任务」始终建模为「被自动化任务」的一个比例。换句话说，其底层假设是：自动化总会减少人类劳动（或至多保持不变）。 按倒序来说： 自动化总会减少人类工作吗？ 以我的经验，答案是否定的。自动化常常为人类创造出成倍增加的工作。 我在这里有完整的论证（https://t.co/Lb7SUCe2pO），说明为什么这在结构上是成立的。但我认为，任何对 AI 经济影响的诚实分析，都必须认真对待「工作创造」这件事。 工作可以被拆解为任务吗？ 可以，这是一个有用的视角。但任务清单只是看待工作的一种方式——它让「一份工作有多少可被自动化」这个问题变得可处理。因此，它是有用的。 但这并非全貌，它只是「在我们提问方式下所呈现出的工作」。 一个好例子：在这个模型下，新任务从何而来？难道只是老板往任务清单里加一条，让员工去完成？那些任务又从何而来？除非你能很好地建模这一点，否则你的分析就缺失了一个关键项（见上面第一点）。 换一种看法，借用维特根斯坦和海德格尔的思路：一份工作是「看见并关心这个世界」的一种特定方式。编辑会注意到护士注意不到的东西，反之亦然。你所关心的，塑造了你会注意到什么、什么看起来值得做、以及什么算做得好。你可以把其中一部分显式化为任务清单。但当你的工具和环境变化时，同样的「关心」会生成全新的任务。这个过程本身就是工作的一部分。 以上这些在这份分析中完全缺失（可以理解，因为按定义它极难建模！）。 但如果你认真对待它，它应当会大幅改变你对「自动化与工作」的看法。
